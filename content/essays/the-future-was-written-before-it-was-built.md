---
title: The Future Was Written Before It Was Built
type: Article
category: Systems Thinking
author: Bell John K
excerpt: What we call innovation today was once just someone’s imagination on paper.
---




Jules Verne and H. G. Wells are not just personal favorites. They belong to a rare group of writers who did more than entertain readers—they changed the way humanity imagined the future. Long before technology gave shape to submarines, time machines, moon voyages, and alien invasions, these writers had already placed such possibilities in the public imagination.

Science fiction is one of the most fascinating literary genres because it stands at the meeting point of wonder and reason. It is a genre that asks a simple but powerful question: what happens next if science, technology, or human ambition moves one step further than the present allows?

That question is what gives science fiction its lasting power. In many other genres, the writer explores the known world. In science fiction, the writer builds a possible world—sometimes dazzling, sometimes terrifying, but always charged with the energy of possibility. That is why sci-fi is not only enjoyable to read; it also stimulates curiosity, challenges assumptions, and invites readers to think beyond the limits of their own time.

Jules Verne understood this power beautifully. He dreamed of a new kind of novel that would combine scientific fact with adventure fiction, and that vision became the foundation of his extraordinary body of work. In novels such as Journey to the Centre of the Earth, From the Earth to the Moon, and Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Sea, Verne wrote with the mind of a patient architect of possibility, grounding wonder in research, detail, and technical plausibility.

Reading Verne feels like listening to a man who believed that the world was still unfinished. His stories suggest that the Earth still had hidden interiors, the oceans still held mysteries, and the sky itself was not a boundary but an invitation. Even when his ideas seemed extravagant to readers of his own century, many of them later appeared strikingly close to real technological developments.

H. G. Wells approached the future differently. He was not only a novelist but also a journalist, sociologist, and historian, and that broader intellectual identity is visible in all his major fiction. If Verne often wrote like an engineer of wonder, Wells wrote like a restless examiner of civilization.

In The Time Machine, The Invisible Man, and The War of the Worlds, Wells did not simply imagine inventions or impossible scenarios for spectacle alone. He used speculative ideas to question class, power, empire, violence, and the fragility of human confidence. His stories were often less about predicting devices and more about exposing the hidden tensions inside society.

This is why Verne and Wells remain such a compelling pair. Verne often looked at science as a pathway to discovery and expansion, while Wells made science fiction a sharper moral instrument, one that could expose the darker consequences of progress and the hidden weaknesses of modern society. One writer gave the genre its sense of technical wonder; the other gave it intellectual urgency.

Together, they helped define two great energies within science fiction. One is the spirit of aspiration: the belief that imagination and science can enlarge human possibility. The other is the spirit of warning: the recognition that every new power also raises new ethical, social, and political questions. The genre became richer because it learned to carry both dreams and doubts at once.

Their legacy did not end with them. Hugo Gernsback helped establish science fiction as an independent literary form, and later writers such as Isaac Asimov, Arthur C. Clarke, and Ray Bradbury expanded the field in different directions, from scientific speculation to social criticism and lyrical visions of technological anxiety.

That continuing tradition explains why science fiction matters far beyond the page. It is no longer just a literary category; it has become a cultural force. Stories once dismissed as impossible began to influence inventors, scientists, filmmakers, and ordinary readers who learned to see tomorrow as something that could be imagined before it was engineered.

Perhaps that is the deepest truth about science fiction. It is not merely about machines, planets, or futuristic gadgets. It is about the human ability to think beyond the visible world and to test the consequences of that thinking in narrative form. In that sense, science fiction is both a mirror and a map: it reflects the anxieties of its age while sketching the outlines of ages yet to come.

That is why Jules Verne and H. G. Wells still feel alive today. They do not survive only as classic authors on library shelves. They survive in every rocket launch, every speculative debate about artificial intelligence, every question about the future of humanity, and every reader who senses that imagination is often the first workshop of reality. The future, after all, was written before it was built.
