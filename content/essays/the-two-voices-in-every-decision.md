There's a voice that speaks up every time I'm about to make a decision. It's quiet but confident. It says: *I've seen this before. I know how this goes.*

That voice is experience. And in most rooms — engineering briefings, strategy meetings, railway operations — it's the loudest one at the table.

I don't distrust it. Experience is genuinely valuable. It builds pattern recognition, sharpens intuition, and gives you a feel for context that no spreadsheet can replicate. After years in electrical engineering at the railway, I've come to respect what accumulated judgment can do — the way a seasoned technician senses trouble before the instruments catch it.

But here's what experience cannot do: it can only speak to what *you* have personally encountered. It is, by definition, a sample of one life.

That's not a small limitation.

## The Problem with Being Good at What You've Done

When you've made a decision before and it worked, the brain files that away as a template. Next time a similar situation appears, it reaches for the template. This is efficient. It's also how biases calcify into conviction.

The decisions that have hurt organizations I've observed weren't made by careless people. They were made by experienced people who trusted their templates a little too much — who had stopped asking whether the situation was *actually* similar or just *looked* similar.

Scientific reasoning is the corrective. Data, controlled observation, measurable evidence — these don't care about your track record. They force you to test the template against reality rather than against memory.

## The Photography Class That Changed How I Think About Learning

There's a well-known experiment that I keep returning to. A photography professor divided his class into two groups at the start of the term.

The first group would be graded purely on **quantity** — how many photographs they submitted. The second would be graded on a single **perfect** photograph, produced over the entire semester.

The intuitive prediction: the group obsessing over one perfect image would produce the best work. More time, more care, more thought.

The actual result: the quantity group won, decisively. The students taking hundreds of photographs were learning through iteration — trying, failing, noticing what failed, adjusting, trying again. They were, in effect, running experiments. The quality group spent so much time theorizing about perfection that they never built the feedback loops that create it.

To me, this isn't just a story about photography or even about practice. It's a story about epistemology — about *how we come to know things*.

The quantity group was building experiential knowledge *through* a scientific process: observation, hypothesis, test, revision. The quality group was relying on reasoning in the abstract, without grounding it in evidence.

Neither instinct nor analysis alone produced mastery. The marriage of the two did.

## Where I've Landed

I'm drawn, by temperament and training, toward the evidence side of this spectrum. When I have a decision in front of me, I want calculations, tested findings, and data I can interrogate. I'm suspicious of conclusions that rest entirely on "in my experience."

But I've also come to see that experience asks the questions that data alone wouldn't think to ask. The seasoned engineer notices the anomaly that triggers the investigation. Intuition is often just a hypothesis wearing comfortable clothes — and hypotheses are the starting point of science, not its enemy.

The balance I try to hold is this:

**Use experience to know *where* to look. Use evidence to know *what* you're actually seeing.**

Experience sets the direction. Scientific thinking validates it, challenges it, and sometimes overturns it entirely. That overturning, uncomfortable as it is, is where real progress lives.

There's a certain humility required to hold both voices at once — to respect what you've lived through without letting it become a ceiling on what you're willing to learn. Real certainty, the kind worth trusting, isn't the certainty of never having been wrong. It's the certainty that comes from having tested your beliefs against the world and updated them honestly.

That, I think, is how experience and evidence stop being rivals and start being collaborators.
