---
title: Eight Beds, Eight Worlds
type: Short Story
category: Meaningful Living
author: Bell John K
excerpt: A Kolkata dormitory. Eight strangers, one room, zero sleep. And
  somewhere in the middle of it all — a British tourist who came to study Indian
  diversity and ended up getting a masterclass.
---
If you ever want proof that India is unlike anywhere else on earth, don't bother with the monuments. Don't book a heritage tour. Just find yourself a budget dormitory in Kolkata, take a bed somewhere in the middle, and wait.

That's what happened to me once. And I've never forgotten it.

The room was simple enough. Eight beds, two rows of four, facing each other like a polite argument. I was there for a short visit — a few days, nothing more. What I witnessed in those nights, though, deserves a permanent record.

Let me introduce the cast.

**Bed 1: The Businessman from Andhra**

By day, this man was immaculate. Safari suit, pressed collar, hair oiled to a mirror finish — the kind of person who walks into a room and makes it feel like a boardroom. Very impressive.

By night, he existed exclusively in a bath towel.

That was it. Just the towel. Sitting on his bed, standing by his bag, walking to the bathroom — bath towel. Whatever transformation happened between that towel and that safari suit each morning, it was nothing short of miraculous. Like a butterfly, but for corporate attire.

**Bed 2: The Sleeping Philosopher**

Next to the businessman was a young man with long hair and a carefully constructed "yo-yo" look — the kind of studied cool that takes considerable effort to maintain. He maintained it beautifully by doing almost nothing. Specifically: sleeping.

I never saw him awake. Not once. He arrived already in a state of horizontal meditation and I believe he left the same way. Maybe he was jet-lagged. Maybe he was enlightened. It's hard to tell the difference.

**Bed 3: My Friend**

A normal human being. Blessedly, reassuringly normal. I will say no more about him because there is nothing funny to report, and that itself was a kind of gift.

**Bed 4: The Night Reader**

Here is where the first row ends, and where the true drama begins.

The Sardarji — middle-aged, unhurried, possessed of a certainty about the world that I found both baffling and admirable — would arrive late. Very late. The exact hour was unpredictable, but the routine was not.

He would enter the room, switch on every single light, settle himself with the deliberate comfort of a man who has earned his rest, and then open a newspaper. Not to skim. Not to check headlines. To *read*. Cover to cover. Column by column. The classifieds, the corrections, the weather for cities he would never visit. All of it, consumed with absolute focus, while eight men tried to sleep around him.

Nobody said a word to him. There was something about his commitment that commanded a kind of helpless respect. He was not rude. He simply operated on a different relationship with time — one where midnight was a perfectly reasonable hour to catch up on the morning news.

When he slept, nobody ever knew. He was like a star that way. Always there when you looked. Gone when you didn't.

**Bed 5: The Quarrelling Bengali**

Across the aisle, the second row began with him.

He was a Bengali gentleman of uncertain occupation and certain habits. Chief among his habits: a bottle, consumed regularly. Chief among his consequences: a phone call to his wife, also consumed regularly.

These calls were loud. They were passionate. They were — and I say this with every charitable interpretation available to me — *not going well.* The conversation would escalate, plateau at volume, descend briefly into silence, and then resume. In between these episodes, he would lean over the edge of his bed and make offerings to the floor.

He was, in his way, a deeply consistent man.

**Bed 6: The Young Lama**

Right next to him — impossibly, almost cosmically next to him — was a young Buddhist monk.

A *lama*. Robes, shaved head, the whole thing. He could not have been older than twenty-five. And he bore his proximity to Bed 5 with a serenity that I can only describe as supernatural. Each incident — the shouting, the vomiting, the general chaos — was met with the same expression: calm eyes, a small smile, a slight nod, as if to say: *yes, this too is the world.*

I've thought about him many times since. Whatever he was practicing, it was working.

**Bed 7: Mine**

I slept here. Or tried to.

**Bed 8: The British Tourist**

And here we arrive at perhaps the most perfect detail in this entire story.

On the last bed was an elderly British gentleman — retired, curious, carrying a small notebook. He had come to India, he told me, to study its diversity. To understand how so many different kinds of people, from so many different backgrounds, could exist together in the same space.

He couldn't have known that Kolkata had prepared for his arrival.

The night it all came together was the night the Bengali slipped.

He had been trying to get upright — some negotiations with gravity that weren't going his way — and in the process, landed squarely on the monk's bed. The monk, startled but unshaken, was now at the bottom of a small human avalanche, still maintaining composure with the patience of someone who had clearly meditated through worse.

I got up. Helped sort out the situation. The monk smiled and thanked me.

From the last bed, I caught the British tourist watching with wide eyes and a pen hovering over his notebook.

We were all still waiting for the Sardarji.

Somewhere around 1 AM, the lights came on.

I've stayed in many places since that trip — hotels, guesthouses, friends' homes. But I've never slept in a room quite like that one. Eight beds, eight completely different human beings, from different states, different faiths, different decades, different relationships with sleep and silence and the night.

India puts you in rooms like that sometimes. Rooms that don't make sense on paper but somehow hold together by morning.

The British tourist wanted to study Indian diversity. I wonder if he wrote about that dormitory. I hope he did.

It was, after all, a first-class classroom.
